One invoice, four desks, nothing lost in between.

A payment request starts as a photo of a bill and ends as a UTR number. In between it passes an approver, an accountant and a payer — each adding the one thing only they can add. This is the concept, drawn as screens, before any of it is built.

01 — The lifecycle

Five stages, and one thing that can happen at any of them

Every request is always in exactly one stage. The stage says who owns it right now, and the whole product is arranged around that single fact: your queue is the set of requests whose stage points at you.

STAGE 1

Raised

Bill photographed or uploaded, amount and vendor read off it and confirmed, department attached, note added.

Requester · own depts
STAGE 2

Awaiting approval

An approver for that department qualifies it, adds notes, and sets the payment cycle: unspecified, immediate, or a named date.

Approver · global or scoped
STAGE 3

With accounts

Head of account, voucher number and the company it books into. Vendor record created or matched. Accounting confirmed.

Accountant · global or scoped
STAGE 4

To pay

Payer verifies vendor bank and KYC against the bill, pays outside the system, and records the reference.

Payer · global or scoped
STAGE 5

Paid

UTR or cheque number on record with the bill. Closed — but the thread stays open forever.

System · archived
Not every request reaches stage 5. An approver has three other answers — return it for correction, hold it because the goods were wrong, or reject it outright — and each leaves the request somewhere different. That is section 04, and it matters more than the happy path.
Query is not a sixth stage — it is a hold. Any desk can raise a query to the requester, the approver, or both. The request keeps its stage and its position in the queue, but is flagged and frozen from advancing until the query is answered. That keeps the queue honest: nothing quietly disappears into a side list.
Department is the axis everything filters on. Every request carries the raiser's department. Every desk holds its role either globally or for a named set of departments, so a queue is always the requests in my stage, in my departments. Within that pool nothing is individually assigned — whoever gets there first owns it. Requesters are the one role that cannot be global: you raise for departments you belong to.
Self-approval is allowed, and recorded. One person may hold several roles. If the same person acts twice on a request, the system does not block it — it stamps the trail with a duplicate-actor note that shows on the record and in the monthly control report.
02 — Mobile, installed as a PWA

The phone is the front door

Capture is the only thing most people will ever do, and they will do it standing next to whoever handed them the bill. One screen, one button, camera opens immediately. Everything else on mobile is checking status and answering someone.

What "PWA" actually buys here, and what it doesn't. Installed to the home screen it gets an app icon, full-screen chrome, camera access through the file input, a local draft queue in IndexedDB, and push notifications — on iOS, push works only once installed to the home screen, and true background upload sync is Android-only. So drafts retry in the foreground on next open rather than pretending to sync in the background. Full plan in section 16.

04 — Rejection, return and hold

"No" is three different answers

A wrong price, defective goods, and a payment that simply shouldn't be made are not the same event, and collapsing them into one Reject button is how a system starts lying to the people reading its reports. Each one has a different owner afterwards, a different way back, and a different meaning at year end.

EXIT A

Approve

Cleared, payment cycle set, moves to accounts.

→ Stage 3
EXIT B

Return for correction

The bill or the request is wrong. Wrong rate, wrong quantity, illegible scan, missing LR or delivery note, wrong department. The requester fixes it and resubmits.

→ Back to requester · reversible
EXIT C

Hold — vendor issue

The paperwork is fine; the goods aren't. Short supply, damage, quality rejection, dispute. Nothing is wrong with the request — it must not be paid yet.

→ On hold · reviewed weekly
EXIT D

Reject

This will not be paid. Unauthorised spend, duplicate of an existing bill, or simply declined. Terminal, with a written reason.

→ Closed unpaid · final
And Query, which is none of these. A query is a question that leaves ownership exactly where it is — the approver still owns the request while waiting for an answer. A return hands ownership back. If you find yourself querying the same thing twice, you meant to return it.

A return keeps its identity. Same reference, same thread, same trail — resubmission is revision 2, with the old bill and the old amounts still readable. Raising a fresh request instead would hide the fact that the first version was wrong, which is exactly the thing an approver most wants to see.
Nothing is declined silently. Every exit notifies the requester with the reason text, by email and push. A rejection the requester learns about by noticing the request went quiet is a rejection that turns into a phone call.
Hold has a review date, or it becomes a graveyard. Held requests are excluded from ageing flags — otherwise the queue turns red for something deliberately parked — but they carry a review date, they reappear on it, and the on-hold list is a standing agenda item. Anything held more than 60 days is escalated to the super admin, because a bill that will never be paid should be rejected honestly, not left pending forever.
Later desks can send back, but not reject. An accountant who can't book a bill returns it to the approver; a payer whose vendor bank doesn't match returns it to accounts. Only an approver can end a request unpaid — the decision not to pay a vendor is an approval decision, wherever it gets noticed.

The re-uploaded rejection

A bill that was declined in March being uploaded again in August is either an honest resubmission after the vendor fixed something, or an attempt to get a second answer from a different approver. The system cannot tell which — so it refuses to let it be either one quietly.

Rejected requests stay in the index forever. Closing a request unpaid removes it from queues, not from the duplicate check. A system that only compares against live requests is a system where the way to get a declined bill paid is to wait.
Rejected as a duplicate is different again. If the earlier request was closed because it duplicated something already paid, re-uploading it is a hard block, not a reconsideration — the check follows the link through to the surviving request and reports that one.
The banner never comes off. "Reconsideration of REQ-2082, rejected 03 Sep" travels with the new request through approval, accounts and payment. The payer, three weeks and four desks later, sees it too.
It gets counted. Resubmissions after rejection are a line in the monthly control report, per department and per vendor. One is a fixed problem. Nine from the same vendor is a conversation someone needs to have.

The guarantee, in the database

-- a bill's identity, normalised before it is ever compared
invoice_key = upper(regexp_replace(invoice_no,'[^A-Za-z0-9]','','g'))
vendor_key  = coalesce(vendor.gstin, vendor.pan, vendor.id)
fy          = financial_year(invoice_date)   -- vendors restart numbering

-- one bill, one payment. not a UI check.
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_payment_per_invoice
  ON request (vendor_key, invoice_key, fy)
  WHERE stage = 'paid';

-- the same bank transaction cannot back two requests
CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_utr_per_payment
  ON payment (upper(reference));

-- every match, every verdict, every override: written down
CREATE TABLE duplicate_check (
  request, matched_request, signals[], score,
  verdict, decided_by, reason, at );

Two people submitting the same bill at the same instant is not caught by a lookup — both queries return nothing before either writes. The unique index is what makes that race safe; the second write fails and its author gets the comparison screen, not an error page.

What goes wrong, and what happens then

  • Extraction failed, no invoice number. The decisive signal is unavailable, so the soft check falls back to vendor + amount + date. The authoritative check re-runs at the accounts desk the moment a human types the number — before a voucher exists.
  • The number is written differently. SAC/25-26/0912, sac 25 26 0912 and SAC-25-26-912 all normalise to the same key. Leading zeros are stripped; a trailing revision suffix is not.
  • The same firm exists twice in the vendor master. Keying on GSTIN or PAN rather than the vendor row means two records for one firm still collide. Merging them is a separate cleanup, not a prerequisite.
  • Two vendors, same invoice number. Common with small vendors. Vendor identity is half the key, so this is not a duplicate and nothing is shown.
  • The vendor restarts numbering each April. Financial year is part of the key, so last year's 0912 and this year's 0912 are different bills.
  • The same bill booked to two companies. Still one bill, still blocked. Company is deliberately not in the key — an invoice is paid by one entity.
  • The match is in a department you can't see. The search ignores your scope, but the result is redacted to reference, date and status, with the name of someone who can see it. Withholding the fact of a duplicate to protect a scope boundary would be the wrong trade.
  • A genuine repeat — same number, same amount, monthly retainer. Overridable by an approver or above, with a reason, stamped in the trail and listed in the duplicate report.
  • The check is wrong and it's blocking a real payment. There is always an override. A control with no way past it stops the business, and then someone pays that vendor outside the system — which is worse than the duplicate.
09 — Advances, part-payments and settlement

A request is no longer one bill and one payment

Allowing advances breaks the assumption the rest of this design rested on. A request can now begin without an invoice, be paid more than once, and stay open after money has moved — waiting for a tax invoice that hasn't arrived. So the request stops being a document with a status and becomes a small ledger: what is owed, what has been paid, what is still due, and what paperwork is still outstanding.

KIND 1

Against an invoice

The original flow. A tax invoice exists on day one, one payment settles it, the request closes when the money moves.

Most requests
KIND 2

Against an invoice, in parts

The invoice exists, but the approver schedules it in instalments — or the payer settles part of it. Open until the balance reaches zero.

Large or disputed bills
KIND 3

Advance, against a quotation

No invoice yet. A quotation, proforma or work order is the supporting document. Money goes out first and the tax invoice is collected afterwards — which is an obligation the system has to chase.

Deposits, mobilisation, custom work
The dangerous case is not the advance — it is the invoice that follows it. The vendor sends the tax invoice weeks later, it reaches someone who never saw the advance, and it gets raised as a fresh request for the full amount. The advance is already paid; now the full invoice is paid too. This is the single most likely way this system pays for something twice, and it is the reason the settlement ledger and the duplicate check have to be the same mechanism rather than two features that happen to sit near each other.

What stops the double payment

  • An approved total is a cap. The sum of every payment against a request cannot exceed it. Exceeding requires a fresh approval, not an override at the payer's desk.
  • An open advance is a duplicate signal. A new request whose vendor has an unsettled advance triggers a sixth verdict — matches an open advance — offering to attach the invoice to the existing request instead of creating a new one. Declining that is possible and is recorded.
  • The invoice can only be consumed once. Attaching a tax invoice to a request writes its number into the same duplicate index a normal invoice request uses, so it can never later be raised on its own.
  • Balance is derived, never typed. Invoice total minus advances applied, calculated at the moment of payment. A payer cannot type ₹8,40,000 into a request that already has ₹3,36,000 against it.
  • Part-payments are a schedule, not free-form. Each instalment has its own reference and value date; the running balance is the request's, not a spreadsheet's.
This makes "paid is final" narrower than it was. An individual payment stays immutable — reference and amount cannot be edited once saved. What is no longer final is the request: it can accept another payment until the balance is zero. Correcting a wrong payment still means a new linked entry, never an edit.
Advances need their own report line. Total advances outstanding, average age, invoices overdue, and advances to vendors with no subsequent invoice at all. That last one is the number an auditor asks for first, and it belongs in the monthly control pack.
A refund is a real outcome. If the final invoice comes in below the advance, the vendor owes money back. The request stays open as refund due with the amount, and closes when the credit is received — not when everyone stops looking at it.
One thing I've left out. Retention money — holding back 5–10% until a warranty period ends — is common in works contracts and is a third kind of balance, held deliberately rather than owed. Say if it applies and I'll model it; it is a small addition on top of this ledger but a large one if bolted on later.

Reports worth building as the data accumulates

Grouped by what has to exist first
From day one
  • Payables ageing — open requests by stage and days waiting, the operational list.
  • Cycle time by stage and department — median and 90th percentile, so one terrible outlier doesn't hide behind a decent median.
  • Spend by department, company, head and vendor — the same bars, four groupings.
  • Control exceptions — the eight lines already on the overview.
  • Accounting reconciliation — vouchers here against vouchers in Tally, by company and series, catching the drift two systems always develop.
  • Advances outstanding — value, age, invoices overdue, and advances with no invoice at all. The line an auditor asks for first.
  • Rejections and holds by reason — which departments return the most bills, and for what.
  • Approver load — requests cleared per approver, and which departments depend on one person.
After about three months
  • Vendor concentration — what share the top ten hold, and which single vendor a department cannot operate without.
  • Price drift — same vendor, same head, rising unit values. The quiet one nobody notices monthly.
  • Requester behaviour — correction rate, duplicate rate, how often their bills are returned. Useful for training, dangerous as a scoreboard.
  • Approver consistency — approve, return, hold and reject rates side by side. Two approvers in one department deciding differently is worth knowing.
  • Extraction value — fields corrected per bill, trending. Tells you whether the model is earning its cost.
  • Duplicate-check precision — blocks versus overrides. A rising override rate means the check is too tight and people are learning to route around it.
After a full year
  • Year on year by head and department — the first report where the numbers mean something on their own.
  • Seasonality — when volume spikes, so approver cover can be planned rather than discovered.
  • Terms adherence per vendor — who we consistently pay late, and which relationships that is quietly costing.
  • Cash outflow forecast — the dated payment cycles already in the queue, projected forward. The report a promoter asks for first.
  • Vendor lifecycle — onboarded, used once, dormant, exited. Thirty-four new vendors of which nine were used once is a procurement question.
Once other data exists
  • Budget versus actual — needs budgets per head and department, which the system does not hold today.
  • Three-way match exceptions — needs purchase orders and goods receipts. The single largest control this design is currently missing.
  • GST input credit reconciliation — invoices booked here against what appears in GSTR-2B. Needs a filing feed.
  • TDS liability by section — needs deduction to be computed rather than typed.

Building all of these now would be a mistake — most need history to say anything true, and a report built on six weeks of data teaches people to distrust the reports. The ones in the first group ship with the product; the rest earn their place when there is something to see.

What gets written down

  • Every stage change — with the before and after stage, the actor, and the reason text where one was required.
  • Every field edit — old value → new value, including the model's extraction and the human's correction of it.
  • Every file event — uploaded, viewed, downloaded, exported, with the checksum.
  • Every comment, query and nudge — edits keep the original text; nothing is silently rewritten.
  • Every access change — role granted, scope widened, role revoked, by whom.
  • Every sign-in and failed sign-in — with device and network, and MFA challenges for payer accounts.
  • Every override — duplicate block overridden, stage forced, redaction lifted, each with its written reason.
  • Every vendor bank change — the single highest-value line in the whole log.
Tamper-evident, not just append-only. Each entry stores the hash of the one before it. A nightly job walks the chain and the console shows the verified-through timestamp. The application's database role has no UPDATE or DELETE grant on the table at all — so changing history means changing the database's own permissions, which is itself an event somewhere else.
The trail outlives the request. Eight years, matching the retention on the bills themselves. Deleting a user deactivates them and keeps every entry they generated — an audit trail with a hole where a departed employee used to be is not an audit trail.
Two people can read the whole log, and both are watched. Super admin sees it in full. Developer sees it with amounts, vendor names and bank details masked. Everyone else sees the trail on requests within their scope — which is most of the value, delivered to the people most likely to notice something odd.
An evidence pack, not a CSV. Exporting for an auditor produces the filtered entries, the chain hashes that prove they weren't edited, the bills referenced, and a cover sheet naming who exported what and when. And that export is itself logged.

16 — Infrastructure

Boring where it can be, deliberate where it can't

Thirty-odd users, a few hundred invoices a month, two-digit gigabytes of scanned bills, and a seven-to-eight year retention duty. That shape does not need distributed anything. It needs one Postgres, one object store, one deployable app, and real care in exactly three places: files, extraction, and the audit trail.

Layer 01

Client

Next.js (App Router)ReactTypeScript

One codebase serves desktop and mobile; the PWA is the same app with a manifest, a service worker and a mobile-first route group — not a second product to keep in sync.

PWA specifics: Serwist service worker, precached shell, IndexedDB draft queue, <input capture> for the camera, client-side downscale to ~2000px before upload so a 12 MP photo doesn't cost a plant worker four minutes on 3G. Background Sync on Android; foreground retry-on-open for iOS, which has no equivalent.

Layer 02

App & API

Next.js server actions + route handlers

No separate API service in v1. Stage transitions live in one server-side module with the permission check inside it, so there is exactly one place where "can this person approve this" is answered.

Split out a standalone API only when a second client appears (a native app, an ERP integration). Not before.

Layer 03

Identity

WorkOS or Auth0 / Clerk

Email link plus Google Workspace SSO, since the org already has Google accounts. Mandatory MFA on any account holding the payer role. Sessions short on desktop, long-lived on the installed PWA — a plant worker should not re-authenticate to photograph a bill.

Roles and scope are ours, in role_grant — never in the identity provider's metadata. That keeps access changes auditable in the same trail as everything else.

Layer 04

Data

PostgresDrizzle

Managed — Supabase, Neon or RDS, in an Indian region for data residency. Scope enforced in the query layer with Postgres row-level security as the backstop, so a mistake in application code cannot leak another department's requests.

Point-in-time recovery on. Nightly logical dump to a separate bucket, because PITR protects against the server dying and not against a bad migration.

Layer 05

Files

S3-compatible object storage Cloudflare R2 / AWS S3

Bills are the asset the business must still have in 2034. Private bucket, uploads via short-lived presigned URLs straight from the phone, reads via signed URLs that expire in minutes. Versioning and object-lock on, so nothing is ever truly deleted by accident.

Server-side: checksum on arrive, PDF first-page render and image thumbnail for the queue views, virus scan before the file is ever served back.

Layer 06

Extraction

Claude with vision Haiku 4.5 for volume, Sonnet 5 on escalation

PDF page or photo in, strict JSON out — vendor, amount, currency, invoice number, invoice date, GSTIN — with a per-field confidence. Vendor name is then fuzzy-matched against the master so the requester sees "matched existing vendor" rather than creating a duplicate.

Two tiers, escalated on confidence rather than only on failure. Every bill goes to claude-haiku-4-5 first — fast and cheap enough that running it on all several hundred bills a month is a rounding error. If any decisive field comes back below its confidence floor, or the JSON doesn't validate, or the call errors, that single bill is re-run on claude-sonnet-5. Escalating on a soft signal rather than a hard failure is the part that matters: a confidently wrong amount is worse than a missing one, so the floor is set high enough that borderline reads get the better model instead of reaching a human as a plausible guess.

Expect the large majority to settle on the first tier — the escalation rate is the metric, and it is on the developer's report. If it climbs, something changed: a new vendor's layout, more phone photos, a worse camera. If it approaches zero, the confidence floor is set too low and Haiku is being trusted with reads it shouldn't have.

Three rules that matter more than either model: extraction runs as a background job and never blocks submit; a failure degrades to empty fields and a visible message, never to silence; and both the model's answer and the human's correction are stored, so accuracy is measurable rather than assumed.

Layer 07

Jobs

pg-boss on the same Postgres or Inngest

Extraction, thumbnails, virus scan, notification fan-out, the nightly ageing sweep that raises red flags and the weekly digest. At this volume a queue in the database you already run beats a second piece of infrastructure to operate.

Layer 08

Accounting export

One-way export to Tally XML / TallyPrime ODBC

Vouchers are created here and pushed out, never pulled back. Each confirmed accounting entry queues an export carrying company, head, voucher number, vendor, amount, TDS and the bill as an attachment; the export's acknowledgement is written back onto the request so a failure is visible rather than assumed.

Two systems holding the same voucher number will drift, so there is a nightly reconciliation report — vouchers here against vouchers there, by company and series, listing anything present in one and not the other. That report is the actual deliverable; the export is just the mechanism.

Deliberately one-way. Reading balances back would make this a second ledger, which is a far larger commitment than the one being made here.

Layer 09

Notifications

ResendWeb Push (VAPID)

Email for every stage handover, query and nudge — the channel that always arrives. Web push for immediacy on installed devices, treated as a bonus rather than a guarantee, because it silently isn't delivered to plenty of phones.

Delivery is verified, not assumed: the health screen shows the actual push acceptance rate. WhatsApp Business is the obvious later addition for nudges — real people in Indian ops read WhatsApp and not email.

Layer 10

Observability

SentryStructured logsUptime check

Plus the in-app health screen from section 12, which is the one a non-engineer can read. Alert on: extraction success below 80%, any request older than its department threshold, queue depth, and failed payments-desk saves.

Layer 11

Hosting

Vercel or a container on AWS ap-south-1

Three environments: local, preview-per-branch with a seeded throwaway database, and production. Every secret in the platform's store, none in the repo, none pasted into chat.

If data residency turns out to be a hard requirement rather than a preference, the whole stack has an ap-south-1 equivalent — this is a deliberate check to make before customer data lands, not after.

Layer 12

Control

Append-only eventsMFA on payer8-year retention

No update or delete grant on the events table, even for the application role. Bank-detail changes, redaction lifts and forced stage moves each notify the super admin. Retention set to eight years to sit safely past the statutory requirement for books and GST records.

Five things the parked build learned the hard way

Reasoning, not code

You asked for a fresh start uninfluenced by the old code, and these are not code. They are five conclusions that each cost a real bug or a real near-miss to reach. Arrive at them again on your own terms — but arrive at them.

  • The role the application queries as must not own the tables. Postgres exempts table owners from row-level security by design, so an app connecting as the owner has RLS switched off and no error to tell you. Two connection strings, two roles, and a test that proves an unscoped query returns nothing. Here the isolation being protected is department and company scope rather than tenant, but the trap is identical.
  • A service worker must not cache page HTML. The old reason was cross-tenant leakage. The reason here is a shared plant or office device where the next person to open the app is a different user — same failure, same fix: cache immutable static assets only, fall back to a generic offline page, never to a copy of where somebody else was.
  • An audit entry must commit in the same transaction as the thing it describes. Otherwise there is a window in which the action exists and its record does not, and that window is exactly where an investigation will land.
  • A notification must never be able to break the thing that triggered it. If sending mail happens inside the approval's transaction, a slow or failing provider rolls back the approval. Bound it with a timeout, never let it throw, and suppress sends under test so a stray key can't fire real mail at invented fixture addresses.
  • An unconfigured integration must fail loudly at the edge, not quietly in the middle. The parked build degrades gracefully everywhere — which is correct behaviour and also why an entire feature could sit dead for months with nothing to show for it. Every optional integration needs a visible status somewhere a human looks. That is what section 12's health screen is for, and it should exist in week one rather than week thirty.

Accounts — reuse or create

  • New Supabase project. Don't share a database with a parked application. An additional project carries a cost on the Pro plan — check it before assuming, and if that decides it, a separate schema in the existing project is an acceptable second-best.
  • New R2 bucket. Same account, new bucket, new scoped token. This time with versioning and object lock on from the start, and a retention policy of eight years.
  • New Vercel project. Same account, region bom1. The parked project stays as it is.
  • Same WorkOS account, new environment — and go straight to live credentials rather than repeating the test-mode-forever pattern.
  • Same Resend account, if one now exists. If not, this is the first thing to create, because it is the dependency most likely to be discovered missing late.
  • New GitHub repository. Branch protection was blocked on the plan before; it still will be. Worth deciding once whether to go Pro or public rather than carrying the same unresolved note forward again.
  • An error tracker and an uptime check, on day one this time.

What does not come across at all

  • The schema. Forty-eight tables built for a different product. The model in section 15 is about fifteen, and that is the point.
  • The vocabulary. Requisition, cost centre, RFQ, goods receipt note, three-way match. This product says request, department, company, head, voucher, payment. Mixing the two is how a clean model turns muddy in month three.
  • The phase structure. Phases 1, 2 and 3 belong to the old scope document. The build order here is the four slices in section 16 — spine, conversation, master, speed.
  • Purchase orders, RFQs, catalogues, budgets, vendor returns, service milestones, the support desk. All deferred by decision, not lost by accident. They exist in the parked repository if any of them ever comes back.
  • The three-way match. Worth saying plainly: parking the old build parks the strongest financial control that was ever written here. Decision 8 defers purchase orders, so the approver's judgement is the only check that what was billed was what was ordered. That is a deliberate trade, and it should be a conscious one every time somebody asks why an invoice was approved.
An honest note on what a fresh start costs. The visible work — screens, flows, tables — is not where the time goes. It goes into the parts that took the parked build several sprints to get right and which look like nothing on a screen: auth wired properly, the two-role database split with a test that proves scoping holds, presigned uploads that survive a phone on a bad connection, a service worker that doesn't leak, an audit row that commits with its mutation. Budget for those honestly. Everything in slice 1 is exactly that work, which is why slice 1 delivers one department and no extraction and is still the most valuable thing on the list.
